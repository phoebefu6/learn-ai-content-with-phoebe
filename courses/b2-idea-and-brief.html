Part 1 · the difference between a topic and an idea

The idea worth scaling 6 min live

Here is the trap: people bring AI a topic - "productivity", "onboarding", "our new feature" - and the model happily returns the average take on it, same as it would for anyone. A topic is a subject. An idea is a subject plus a claim: an insight, a point of view, or a tension you actually believe. AI is brilliant at generating and pressure-testing idea candidates, but the insight at the centre has to be yours. That is the one thing on this whole course the model genuinely cannot supply.

LiveTopic vs idea - the test that saves you from slop3 min▶

The quickest way to tell them apart: a topic can be argued by anyone, an idea has a point of view that someone could disagree with. "Content marketing" is a topic. "Most content teams are drowning because they optimised for volume, not memory" is an idea - it makes a claim, and it could be wrong. That edge is exactly what makes it worth reading. If your line could sit on any brand's blog unchanged, it is a topic, and the model will draft it into perfect, forgettable slop.

  • A topic is safe; an idea takes a side. If nobody could disagree, nobody will remember it.
  • The insight is yours. AI can list twenty angles on a topic, but it cannot tell you which one you actually believe from experience.
  • One real idea beats ten topics. Distinctiveness beats volume - the b1 rail, applied at the source.
★ Try it now (any chat AI) - topic vs ideaTake the topic "remote work". Give me 5 versions as a plain topic line, then 5 versions rewritten as an idea that takes a clear point of view someone could disagree with. Then tell me what makes the second set riskier - and more memorable.
Real world

The post that finally landed. A creator published "5 tips for better meetings" to silence for months. Then she wrote one line she actually believed - "most meetings are a failure of writing, not talking" - and built a single piece around it. It took a side, so people argued in the comments, quoted it, and shared it. Same topic. The difference was an idea.

Self-studyThree shapes of a strong idea2 min read▶

A strong idea usually takes one of three shapes. Any of them beats a bare topic.

  • An insight - something true that is not obvious: "onboarding fails in the first five minutes, not the first week".
  • A point of view - a stance you will defend: "we should ship fewer posts and edit them harder".
  • A tension - two true things that pull against each other: "AI makes content faster and makes it forgettable".

When you mine ideas later, you are hunting for one of these. If your candidate is none of them, it is still a topic - keep digging.

Part 2 · the one page that turns an idea into content only you could make

The content brief 6 min live

An idea in your head is fragile - it drifts into the average the moment you start drafting. The brief is what pins it down. It is a short, plain document that captures the idea, who it is for, the angle, the proof, the format, the voice, and the constraints. Get these seven right and any draft that follows is already halfway to yours. AI can draft the brief in seconds from your notes, but the two rows it cannot fill for you are the insight and the proof - those are yours.

Brief elementWhat goes in it
IdeaThe insight or point of view in one sentence - the claim someone could disagree with. Yours.
AudienceWho specifically this is for, and what they already believe or struggle with.
AngleThe way in - the hook or frame that makes the idea land for this audience.
Proof / specificsThe real examples, numbers, or stories that make it credible and unmistakably yours. Yours.
FormatArticle, post, thread, newsletter, script - and roughly how long.
VoiceHow it should sound - two or three adjectives, plus a sample of your writing to match.
ConstraintsWhat to avoid, what is confidential, disclosure or rights notes, the deadline.
LiveWhy the brief is the anti-slop move3 min▶

Slop happens when you hand the model a thin prompt and let it fill the gaps with the average. The brief closes those gaps before drafting starts. When the idea is a real claim, the audience is specific, and the proof is your own, the model has nowhere to be generic - you have already made every decision that matters. The draft becomes execution, not invention. This is the same "the edit is the value" habit from b1, moved one step earlier: decide the substance up front, so editing is polishing, not rescuing.

The two rows only you can fill Let AI draft audience, angle, format, and voice from your notes. But the idea (the insight) and the proof (your real specifics) are yours - if you outsource those, you have written a brief for slop.
Self-studyCadence's brief - a worked example2 min read▶

Cadence is the sample product whose content engine this track builds. A filled brief might read:

  • Idea: teams adopt Cadence and then quietly go back to spreadsheets - because the problem was never the tool, it was the ritual around it.
  • Audience: ops leads who bought a tool that their team stopped using by month three.
  • Angle: "the tool was never the problem" - name the uncomfortable pattern out loud.
  • Proof: the real drop-off curve from month one to month three; one customer's actual "we went back to Sheets" quote (with permission).
  • Format: a 700-word article, plus a short post to tease it.
  • Voice: plain, warm, a little contrarian - like a smart colleague, not a brand.
  • Constraints: no invented stats; anonymise the customer; disclose nothing confidential.

Notice the idea takes a side and the proof is specific and real. That is a brief the model cannot slop up.

Part 3 · use AI to expand, then you pick the one with a real point of view

Mining ideas with AI - without the slop 5 min live

Here is the move that keeps AI on the right side of the slop line: never ask it for ideas from nothing. Feed it your raw material - your notes, your half-formed opinions, the thing a customer said last week, the pattern you keep noticing - and ask it to expand that into idea candidates. It becomes a thinking partner that multiplies what is already yours, instead of a slop machine returning the internet's average take. Then you do the one thing it cannot: pick the candidate with a genuine insight.

LiveExpand your material, then you choose3 min▶

The difference between generic-topic slop and real ideas is the input. "Give me content ideas about productivity" gets you the average of everyone. "Here are my messy notes from three client calls - find the tensions and non-obvious patterns, and turn each into an idea that takes a point of view" gets you candidates rooted in something real. The model widens; you decide. Reject anything that could have been written about any company - keep only what carries your fingerprint.

  • Bring raw material, not a blank topic. Notes, transcripts, opinions, a screenshot of a customer message - the more specific, the less generic the output.
  • Ask for tensions and non-obvious patterns, not "10 ideas". You want the shapes from Part 1: insight, point of view, tension.
  • You pick, and you say why. The candidate you choose should be one you could defend from experience. If none qualify, feed it more of your material.
★ Try it now (any chat AI) - mine, do not inventHere are my raw notes: [paste 5-10 messy lines - things you have noticed, said, or heard]. Do not add facts. Find the tensions and non-obvious patterns in what I already believe, and turn each into a candidate idea that takes a clear point of view. Give me 6, then tell me which 2 are riskiest - those are usually the memorable ones.
The slop test for an idea Before you commit: could this exact idea appear on a competitor's blog, unchanged? If yes, it is a topic - keep mining. If it could only come from you, you have your idea.
Demo 1 of 2

Mine your ideas, pick the one with a POV ★ 13 min · everyone builds

Turn your own experience into idea candidates with AI, then do the human part: pick the one with a genuine insight and sharpen it to a single sentence. This sentence becomes the top line of your Cadence brief.

Brain-dump 5-10 messy lines from your own world - things you have noticed, opinions you hold, a customer quote, a pattern that annoys you. Do not tidy them.

Paste them into ChatGPT or Claude with the mining prompt below. Ask for candidate ideas that take a point of view, plus which are the riskiest.

Read the candidates critically. Reject every one that could appear on a competitor's blog unchanged. Circle the one you could defend from experience.

Sharpen it to one sentence with a claim someone could disagree with. Cut hedging ("can", "might", "some"). Make it a line you would put your name on.

Run the slop test: could only you have written this? If yes, you have your core idea. Keep it open - Demo 2 wraps a brief around it.

The lazy prompt - invites slop (do not use)Give me 10 content ideas about productivity for a SaaS company.
★ The mining prompt (bring your own material)Here are my raw notes: [paste 5-10 messy lines]. Do not add facts, stats, or examples of your own. 1) Find the tensions and non-obvious patterns in what I already wrote. 2) Turn each into a candidate idea that takes a clear point of view - a claim someone could disagree with, not a neutral topic. 3) Give me 6 candidates, then flag the 2 riskiest. I will pick the one I can defend and sharpen it myself.
Why you sharpen it by hand The model can list candidates, but the final one-sentence idea is a judgment call about what you believe - that is the insight the whole brief hangs on, and it has to be yours.
Demo 2 of 2

Write Cadence's content brief ★ 8 min · build your own

Wrap your sharpened idea in a full brief for Cadence - idea, audience, angle, proof, format, voice, constraints. AI drafts the scaffolding from your idea; you fill and own the insight and the proof. Keep this file: b3 feeds it straight into the repurposing simulator.

Give the AI your one-sentence idea from Demo 1, plus a couple of lines about Cadence and who you are writing for.

Ask it to draft the seven-part brief. Let it propose audience, angle, format, and voice - it is good at these from your idea.

Now take the pen. Replace the idea row with your exact sentence, and fill the proof row with real specifics only you have - a number, a quote, a story. No invented facts.

Tighten the constraints: what to avoid, what is confidential, any disclosure or rights notes, the deadline. This is where the b1 rails live in the brief.

Save it as your Cadence brief. You will hand this same brief to the b3 simulator and build long-form, social, and visual from it in later sessions.

★ The brief-drafting promptDraft a one-page content brief around this idea: [paste your one-sentence idea]. Product context: Cadence, [one line on what it is]. Writing for: [audience]. Structure it as seven short rows: Idea · Audience · Angle · Proof/specifics · Format · Voice · Constraints. Rules: propose Audience, Angle, Format, and Voice from my idea. Leave Idea exactly as I wrote it. For Proof, write [I fill this] - do not invent facts, stats, quotes, or examples. Keep it plain and one page.
Real world

The brief that outlived the draft. A content lead started writing every piece from a one-page brief instead of a blank page. The first draft got faster, but the real win came later: when she repurposed the piece into a thread, a newsletter, and a talk, the brief kept all of them on the same idea and proof. One sharp brief powered a month of aligned content. That is exactly what b3 does with yours.
