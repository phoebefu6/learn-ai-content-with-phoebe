Part 1 · the workflow that keeps long-form yours

Outline, draft, edit 6 min live

Long-form works as three moves. AI is genuinely great at the first two - it can outline a shape and produce a fast draft from your brief in seconds. But the draft it hands back is a generic 70%: fluent, structured, and forgettable. The edit is where you make it specific, true, and yours. And the fastest way to get a draft worth editing is to teach the model your voice with two or three samples before it writes a word - few-shot, not from memory.

LiveTeach voice with samples, not adjectives3 min▶

Telling the model "write in a warm, confident voice" gets you the average of warm and confident - which is to say, slop. Showing it two or three real samples of your writing gets you something that actually sounds like you. This is few-shot prompting: examples beat instructions every time, because the model can match a pattern far better than it can interpret a word. Paste your best paragraphs, say "match this voice", then let it draft. You have moved the draft from generic to nearly-yours before you have edited a single line.

  • Show, do not tell. Two or three of your real paragraphs teach voice better than any list of adjectives.
  • The outline is cheap to fix. Argue with the outline before you draft - reordering a draft costs far more than reordering a list.
  • The draft is raw clay. Never ship the AI draft. It is the fast 70%; the edit is where the piece earns the reader.
★ Try it now (any chat AI) - few-shot voiceHere are two paragraphs I wrote: [paste]. Study the rhythm, sentence length, and how I open and close. Now write a new 150-word paragraph on [topic] that matches that voice. Do not add facts I did not give you.
Real world

The newsletter that stopped sounding like a robot. A writer kept getting drafts that were technically fine and completely characterless. She stopped describing her voice and started pasting three of her own past issues as examples. The next draft had her cadence, her asides, her way of starting mid-thought. Same model, same topic - the difference was showing it who she was instead of telling it.

Self-studyWhy the edit is the real writing2 min read▶

In long-form, the edit is not clean-up - it is where the piece is actually written. The AI draft gives you a structure and a rough surface. The edit does the four things that make it worth publishing:

  • Voice: rewrite the openings and transitions in your own cadence - that is where AI tells hide.
  • Specificity: replace at least one vague passage with a real example, number, or story only you have.
  • Point of view: sharpen the claim so the piece takes the side your b2 idea promised.
  • Truth: verify every fact, stat, and quote - or cut it.

Do these four and a generic draft becomes a piece that could only be yours. Skip them and you have published fast, fluent slop.

Part 2 · kill the tells, keep the specifics

On-brand + anti-slop 6 min live

AI-written long-form has tells - the giveaways that make a reader think "a machine wrote this" and stop trusting it. They are predictable, which means they are fixable. On brand means two things at once: it sounds like you (voice, taught with samples) and it serves the reader (one idea per section, skimmable, no filler). The anti-slop edit is mostly a hunt for tells, replacing each with a real specific.

The AI tellThe fix
Generic opener - "In today's fast-paced world..."Open on a specific: a moment, a number, a real line someone said.
Hedging - "can help", "may", "some would argue"Commit to the claim your b2 idea makes. Take the side.
Listicle sameness - every section the same shapeVary rhythm: one idea per section, some short, some with a story.
Empty summary - "In conclusion, it is important to..."End on a real takeaway or a line only you would write.
Vague nouns - "solutions", "leverage", "in order to"Name the actual thing; use plain verbs. Cut the corporate fog.
No proof - confident but nothing concreteAdd one real example, number, or story per section.
LiveStructure for the reader, not the word count3 min▶

Long-form fails when it is structured to fill a length instead of to be read. The fix is discipline the reader can feel: one idea per section, a clear line of argument, and a shape someone can skim and still follow. AI happily pads - it will give you five sections that all say the same thing in slightly different words. Your edit cuts that to the sections that each add something, and makes every heading earn its place.

  • One idea per section. If two sections make the same point, merge them and cut the weaker.
  • Skimmable by design. Headings that carry meaning, short paragraphs, no wall of text - respect the reader's time.
  • Keep the specifics, cut the filler. Every sentence that is not a specific or a real point is a candidate for deletion.
The one-specific rule No section ships without at least one real specific - a number, an example, a story, a named thing. It is the single fastest way to move a section from slop to substance.
Self-studyThe read-aloud test2 min read▶

Before you publish, read the piece aloud. It is the cheapest slop detector there is. The tells announce themselves the moment you hear them:

  • If a sentence sounds like a brand brochure, it is a tell - rewrite it the way you would say it to a colleague.
  • If you run out of breath, the sentence is too long - break it.
  • If a paragraph could be deleted and nothing is lost, delete it.
  • If you cannot point to one real specific on the page, you have not finished editing.

Reading aloud catches in thirty seconds what re-reading silently hides for hours.

Part 3 · same method, different container

Scripts + newsletters 5 min live

A newsletter and a video or podcast script are still long-form - the structure differs, the edit discipline does not. Outline, draft with few-shot voice, then edit for voice, specifics, point of view, and truth. What changes is the shape the reader or listener needs; what stays is that AI drafts fast and you own the version that ships.

LiveNewsletters and scripts, the same spine3 min▶

A newsletter is an article with a relationship - it opens like a note to someone you know, moves fast, and earns the next open by being useful, not by being long. A script is written for the ear, not the eye - shorter sentences, spoken rhythm, a clear beat structure so a listener never gets lost. Both come from the same b2 idea and brief, and both live or die on the same edit. Draft the shape with AI; make it yours by hand.

  • Newsletter: a personal open, one idea, skimmable middle, a single clear takeaway. Write to one reader, not a list.
  • Script: write for the ear - short lines, spoken cadence, a hook in the first ten seconds, beats a listener can follow.
  • Same edit either way: voice via samples, one real specific per section, sharpen the POV, mark and check every claim.
★ Try it now (any chat AI) - reshape, do not re-slopHere is my article draft: [paste]. Reshape it into a 200-word newsletter open - personal, written to one reader, one idea, ending on a single takeaway. Keep my specifics. Mark [verify] on any claim. Do not add facts.
Real world

One idea, three containers. A creator took a single b2 idea and shipped it as an article, a newsletter, and a five-minute video script in an afternoon. AI reshaped the structure for each; she re-edited the voice and kept the same real proof throughout. Readers, subscribers, and viewers all met the same sharp idea in the form that fit them. The method did not change - only the container did.

Demo 1 of 2

Draft a Cadence piece with few-shot voice ★ 13 min · everyone builds

Take your b2 brief and draft a Cadence article or newsletter section - taught in your voice with real samples, and marked up for fact-checking as you go. You will edit it hard in Demo 2.

Open your b2 Cadence brief. Paste the idea, audience, angle, proof, and format into ChatGPT or Claude.

Give it two or three real paragraphs of your own writing as voice samples. Say "match this voice - rhythm, openings, how I close".

Ask for an outline first. Argue with it: reorder, cut, make sure each section carries one idea. Only then ask it to draft.

Instruct it to write [verify] next to any fact, stat, or quote it introduces - and never to invent proof. Your real specifics from the brief go where you marked them.

Read the draft once. Do not fix it yet. Just notice where it sounds like you and where the AI tells crept in - that is your Demo 2 target list.

The thin prompt - drafts pure slop (do not use)Write a 700-word article about how Cadence helps teams stay productive.
★ The few-shot draft promptWrite from this brief: [paste b2 brief - idea, audience, angle, proof, format]. Match my voice - here are 2 samples of my writing: [paste]. First give me an outline: one idea per section, in a logical argument. Wait for my edits. Then draft to that outline. Rules: use only the proof I gave you. Write [verify] next to any claim, stat, or quote. Do not invent facts. Skimmable - short paragraphs, headings that carry meaning. This is a first draft I will edit, not the final.
Why mark [verify] as you draft Fact-checking after the fact means hunting for buried claims. Marking them as they appear turns the fact-check into a checklist - and makes the rail-one promise ("no fabricated facts") something you can actually enforce.
Demo 2 of 2

Run the anti-slop edit pass ★ 8 min · build your own

This is where the piece becomes yours. Take the Demo 1 draft and run a real edit: cut the tells, add a real specific, sharpen the POV, and clear every [verify]. Use AI to find the slop, then do the edit by hand.

Ask AI to flag the tells - generic openers, hedging, listicle sameness, empty summaries, vague nouns - but tell it not to rewrite. You want a list, not a new draft.

Rewrite the opening and the closing in your own voice. These carry the most tells and matter the most - a person opens on a specific, not "in today's world".

Add at least one real specific to every thin section - a number, an example, a story only you have. Apply the one-specific rule.

Sharpen the claim so the piece clearly takes the side your b2 idea promised. Cut the hedging around it.

Clear every [verify]: confirm the fact, source it, or cut it. Then read the whole thing aloud once - fix anything that sounds like a brochure. Ship that.

★ The anti-slop edit prompt (then edit by hand)Here is my draft: [paste]. Do not rewrite it. 1) List every AI tell to cut: generic openers, hedging, listicle sameness, empty summaries, vague nouns like "leverage" or "solutions". 2) Flag every section with no real specific - where I must add a number, example, or story. 3) List every claim still marked [verify] that I must confirm or cut. Be blunt about what reads as AI. I will do the edit myself.
Real world

The eight-minute edit that changed the piece. A writer ran a fluent AI draft through one anti-slop pass: cut two generic openers, replaced a vague claim with a real customer number, sharpened a hedge into an actual opinion, and killed one invented stat that had slipped in. Eight minutes. The piece went from "clearly AI" to "clearly hers" - and it was the specific and the opinion, not the length, that readers responded to.

Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 30-45 min total

  • Finish both demos if you did not complete them live - especially the anti-slop edit pass. Watching a fluent draft become yours is the lesson.
  • Build a reusable voice-sample block: paste your three best paragraphs into a note you can drop into any drafting prompt.
  • Take your Cadence draft and reshape it into a newsletter open using the Part 3 prompt. Keep the specifics, re-check the claims.
  • Run the read-aloud test on one real piece this week. Note every sentence that sounded like a brochure - those are your personal tells.
  • Optional: keep a running list of the AI tells you cut most often. That list becomes your default edit pass, formalised in b9.
