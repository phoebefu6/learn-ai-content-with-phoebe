Part 0

Why video and audio are their own session

Video and voice are where AI feels most like magic - a brief becomes a talking avatar, a script becomes a narrated clip in minutes. That leverage is real. So is the exposure: a voice or a face is a person's identity, and synthetic media carries likeness, copyright, deepfake, and disclosure risk that text never did. This is the one session where the rights rail is loud on purpose. The move is the same as every session before it: the story and the edit stay human, and here so does the question of whose voice and face you are allowed to use.

Part 1 · covers script-and-storyboard-before-generation

Script and storyboard first, generate second 6 min live

Great AI video does not start in a video tool. It starts in a document: a written script and a storyboard. The generators - Runway-class video, ElevenLabs-class voice - are the production layer, and they are only as good as the story you hand them. Skip the writing and you get slick, generic clips nobody finishes. Write first, and the AI has something worth producing.

LiveThe script and the storyboard carry the piece3 min▶

A short video is a tiny story with a job to do. The script decides the words and the beats; the storyboard decides what the viewer sees in each of them. Both are cheap to write and expensive to fix after generation, so you do them first, on paper, before a single clip is rendered. AI can help you draft both - it is good at structure and options - but you pick the hook, the pacing, and the payoff.

  • Script = the spine. Hook in the first two seconds, one clear value beat, one call to action. Short lines, spoken-word rhythm, no filler.
  • Storyboard = the eyes. Shot by shot: what is on screen, the framing, the on-screen text, the transition. This is your shot list for the generator.
  • Generate against a plan. With script and storyboard in hand, the video and voice tools produce to a brief instead of guessing - far less slop, far fewer regenerations.
★ Try it now (any chat AI) - a 3-beat spineDraft a 45-second short-video script on [topic] with three beats: a 2-second hook, one value beat, one call to action. Spoken-word rhythm, short lines. Give me two hook options. I will pick and edit - do not pad it.
Real world

The clip that was beautiful and unwatched. A team generated a run of polished AI videos straight from one-line prompts. They looked great and nobody finished them - no hook, no point, no reason to stay. When they wrote a real script and storyboard first and let the tools produce to it, completion rates jumped. The generator was never the problem; the missing story was.

Self-studyA short-video script skeleton you can reuse2 min read▶

Pin this shape for any 30-60 second short. It is the same hook-value-CTA spine you learned for social, tuned for the ear and the eye.

  • 0-2s hook: the line or image that stops the scroll. Concrete, specific, a little tension.
  • 2-40s value: one idea, delivered in short spoken lines, each paired with a shot and on-screen text.
  • 40-45s CTA: one action - follow, read the full piece, try it. Not three.
  • Under every line: a storyboard cell - shot, framing, on-screen text, transition - so production has no guesswork.
Part 2 · the rights rail, loud

AI video + voice - with the rights rail loud 6 min live

Text-to-video, AI avatars, text-to-speech, and voice cloning are powerful and legally loaded. A voice and a face are a person's identity, protected by likeness and right-of-publicity law; footage and music carry copyright; and synthetic media of a real person is where deepfake harm and disclosure rules live. This is not a footnote here - it is the session. Never clone a real person's voice or face without clear permission, and disclose synthetic media where it matters.

LivePowerful tools, real exposure - the rules that keep you safe3 min▶

The generators do not check rights for you - that is your job, and in video and voice the stakes are higher than anywhere else in this course. The rule of thumb is simple: if a real person's identity is involved, you need their permission in writing, and if the media is synthetic, you disclose it.

  • Voice and likeness are the person's. Cloning a real voice or generating a real face without documented, specific permission is a likeness and right-of-publicity problem - even if the tool makes it a one-click move.
  • Inputs carry copyright. Footage, images, and music you feed in, or that a tool trained on, can be someone else's IP. Use rights-clear or licensed material and check the tool's commercial terms.
  • Deepfake risk is reputational and legal. Realistic synthetic people are the highest-risk output you can make. When in doubt, do not.
  • Disclose synthetic media. Where AI-generated or AI-altered people, voices, or endorsements appear, label them - platform rules and FTC-style disclosure are the norm, not the exception.
The one-line gate for video and voice "Whose voice and face is this, do I have written permission, and have I disclosed that it is synthetic?" If any answer is no, it does not publish. This is rail two at its loudest.

When a row is uncertain, the answer is stop and clear it - not generate and hope. This table is your pre-publish checklist for every AI video and voice piece.

Part 3 · multimodal from one idea

Repurpose an existing piece into video 4 min live

You do not start from a blank page. The Cadence long-form article you wrote in b4 is already a source of stories, and one idea can become text, social, and now video. Turning a written piece into a short-video script is the fastest, safest way onto the frontier - the thinking is done, you are just changing the medium.

LiveFrom one article to a short-video script2 min▶

Take the strongest single idea from the b4 article - one insight, one story, one number - and rebuild it for the ear and eye. A 1,200-word article is not a script; you extract its best 45 seconds and rewrite for spoken rhythm and on-screen visuals. Same idea, native to video. This is the multimodal-from-one-idea move you met in b3, now pointed at the highest-leverage format.

  • Extract, do not narrate. Pull the one beat that carries on screen; do not read the article aloud.
  • Rewrite for the ear. Short spoken lines, a hook up front, one CTA - not article prose.
  • Storyboard the visuals. Each spoken line gets a shot and on-screen text; that is your production brief.

Script + storyboard a Cadence short ★ 13 min · everyone builds

Build the frontier piece from something you already have. Take an existing Cadence idea and turn it into a 30-60 second short-video script with a shot list and a storyboard - hook, value, CTA, ready to hand to a generator.

Pick one Cadence idea you have already used - from the brief in b2, the repurposing run in b3, or the b4 article. One idea, one clear point.

Ask a chat AI for a 45-second script with the three beats: a 2-second hook (give yourself two options), one value beat, one CTA. Spoken-word rhythm, short lines.

Ask it to add a storyboard cell under each line: shot, framing, on-screen text, transition. That is your shot list.

Now edit by hand: pick the hook, cut any filler, replace one generic line with a real Cadence specific, and make sure the CTA is a single action.

Read the script out loud against the storyboard. If it does not sound like a person and read cleanly shot by shot, fix it before any tool sees it.

★ The script + storyboard prompt (then edit by hand)Turn this Cadence idea into a 45-second short-video script: [idea]. Structure: 2-second hook, one value beat, one call to action. Spoken-word rhythm, short lines, no filler. Give me two hook options. Under every spoken line, add a storyboard cell: - shot (what is on screen) - framing (wide / close / text-only) - on-screen text - transition to the next shot Keep it to one clear idea. I will pick the hook and edit for voice.
Why the storyboard is not optional The storyboard is what makes AI video production predictable. Hand a generator a script with no shot list and it improvises - that is where slop comes from. A shot-by-shot plan turns generation into execution.
Demo 2 of 2

Voiceover plan + a rights & disclosure check ★ 8 min · build your own

Before that short gets a voice, plan it and clear it. You will decide how the piece is narrated and run a synthetic-media rights and disclosure check you could defend to a client or a platform.

Decide the voice: your own recorded voice, a licensed synthetic voice from the tool, or - only with written consent - a cloned voice. Write down which and why.

Answer the three rights questions in writing: whose voice is this, whose face or likeness appears, and is each one cleared with documented permission.

Check the inputs: is the music licensed, is any footage rights-clear, does the tool's commercial-use term cover what you are doing.

Decide disclosure: if the voice or any person is synthetic, write the label you will show and where it goes. Where required, it is not optional.

Keep the completed check with the piece. If any answer is "not cleared", the piece does not publish until it is.

★ The voiceover + rights-check promptHelp me plan and clear the voiceover for a short video. Context: the narration option is [my own voice / a licensed synthetic voice / a cloned voice with written consent]; on-screen people are [none / stock avatar / a real person]; music is [licensed / none]; the tool is [name]. Produce a one-page check I can keep on file: 1) Whose voice + whose face, and is each cleared with documented permission? 2) Are all inputs (music, footage) rights-clear or licensed? 3) Does the tool's commercial-use term cover this? 4) Is synthetic media disclosed - what label, shown where? Flag anything that is "not cleared" as a blocker. Plain language.
Real world

The voice that was not theirs to use. A team cloned a well-known creator's voice for a promo because the tool made it a two-minute job. It was fast, convincing, and a right-of-publicity problem the moment it published - a takedown and an apology followed. A thirty-second rights check would have caught it. On the frontier, the check is the work.
